Validation in new patients and updating

Temporal validation in a later recruitment period and external validation in another population: the variable check, case-mix, performance, calibration and model updating.

Question. The model was developed in patients recruited in 2012–2016. Does it hold for the patients recruited in 2017–2019 (temporal validation)? And in a cohort from another population (external validation)? If its risks are wrong there, can they be fixed without building a new model?

Step 1: the panel

The model of the selection example, without selection (the main effects and the age × sex interaction), and in the Validation box:

The model is developed on the 2012–2016 rows only; the 2017–2019 rows and the external file are predicted with it unchanged.

R syntax 
r <- cpLogistic(data = heart, dep = "event10", eventLevel = "yes",
                covs = c("sbp", "chol", "bmi"), splineVars = "age",
                factors = c("sex", "smoking", "diabetes"),
                covScales = list(list(var = "sbp", scale = "ten")),
                interactions = list(list("age", "sex")),
                splitVar = "period", splitLevel = "2017-2019",
                extFile = list(path = "heart_external.csv"),
                updating = TRUE, calPlot = TRUE, explain = TRUE)

An external file is never in exactly the same format. clinpred matches its columns by name ignoring case (Age, SEX), its factor levels ignoring case (Female), and reports what it could not match: 12 patients with smoking unknown, a level the model does not know, are set to missing; some blood pressures lie outside the range the model was developed on (the model is then extrapolating). Rows with a missing predictor are excluded, as in development.

Note. Membership c-statistic: how well the predictors and the outcome distinguish validation from development rows (Debray et al. 2015). Near 0.5: similar case-mix (reproducibility); high: different populations (transportability). A larger SD of the linear predictor means a more heterogeneous case-mix, which by itself raises the AUC.

The later period looks like the development data: same distributions, same spread of the linear predictor, and a membership c-statistic of 0.544 — a model can hardly tell the two apart (Debray et al. 2015). Only the event rate is lower. The external cohort is older (mean age 66.14), smokes more and has higher blood pressure; its membership c of 0.696 says it is a different population. Validating there tests transportability, not just reproducibility.

Note. The development model is applied unchanged to the validation data. A calibration-in-the-large far from 0 means the overall risk differs between the populations; a slope below 1, that the predictions are too extreme there.

Later period. Discrimination holds (AUC 0.711), but the model predicts too many events: O/E = 0.71 (0.59–0.86), a calibration-in-the-large of -0.42 on the log-odds scale. Risk factors act the same, but the baseline risk has fallen — typical of temporal validation, and the reason risk calculators are recalibrated periodically.

External cohort. Discrimination falls (AUC 0.663), the model under-predicts (O/E = 1.26) and its predictions are too extreme there (slope 0.80): the effects of the risk factors are weaker in that population.

Note. Recalibration re-estimates the intercept and the slope of the original prediction; intercept and slope: of the original linear predictor (log-odds) in the updated model. Each test compares a method with model revision (likelihood ratio); the closed testing procedure (Vergouwe et al. 2017) keeps the first method, from the original model up, not significantly worse than revision at α = 0.05 (✓). Performance of an updated model is measured in the data used to update it, so it is optimistic, most of all for revision.

For each validation set the table fits the three updating methods and the closed testing procedure (Vergouwe et al. 2017) chooses (✓):

  • External cohort: the original model and recalibration in the large are significantly worse than revision; recalibration (new intercept 0.09 and slope 0.80) is not, so it is chosen. Two parameters fix most of the miscalibration (ICI from 0.055 to 0.021); re-estimating all 14 coefficients is not needed.
  • Later period: the procedure keeps the original model, although calibration-in-the-large is clearly off. The first test compares the original model with revision, a 14-parameter change, and is not significant (p = 0.069): the one-parameter shift is diluted among the 14. The closed procedure protects against updating on noise, at the cost of power. Here the O/E ratio and its confidence interval show a real shift that a new intercept corrects (ICI from 0.050 to 0.014); report both and decide with clinical judgement.

The performance of an updated model is measured in the data that updated it, so it is optimistic; a recalibrated model should itself be validated before use.
